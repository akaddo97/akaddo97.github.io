What gets taught

There is a real curriculum behind this, six weeks of it. Fifteen topics, in four tracks, in the order below. What changes per person is the lane it gets applied to, not the syllabus.

The wider curriculum spine runs to ninety-four topics. This course teaches fifteen of them. The technical foundations track, the maths, GPU architecture, transformers, and backpropagation, is deliberately left out: this is a tooling-fluency course, not a machine-learning course.

The six weeks

  1. Week 1 Kickoff and the tooling. NotebookLM, Deep Research, Perplexity, and what a large language model is actually doing.
  2. Week 2 Designing your own Deep Research prompts, and how AI search and retrieval works underneath.
  3. Week 3 Prompt depth and iteration, plus enough data science to read what comes back critically.
  4. Week 4 Metaprompting, building agents and tool use, workflow compression, and context management.
  5. Week 5 Knowledge graphs as a concept, and why capturing your own data compounds.
  6. Week 6 Knowledge graphs built for real, with RAG and retrieval wired in, closing the loop on your own work.

Before week 1 there is a warm-up: a piece of real research in your own field, so the tooling has something true to bite on from the first session.

For teams

Team fluency work runs differently. It starts from the job you already do: we go through your actual workflow, find where the time goes, and rebuild those steps around the AI that exists this week. You keep working tools at the end, not notes.

It suits professionals, SMEs, and enterprises, including teams with no engineers in them.

The upskilling cohort

I also run a small AI upskilling cohort, teaching young professionals practical AI skills. It is selective and free for the people I take on. Members commit to an hour a week, do the homework, and report back.

It runs as one lane per person, built around the work they actually do, rather than as one syllabus everybody sits through. Six participants and one peer are running now.
